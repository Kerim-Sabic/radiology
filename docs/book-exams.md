# Book exams (chapters 1 to 13)

One exam for each of chapters 1 to 13 of *Learning Radiology: Recognizing the Basics* (5th ed.), at `/book-exams`. Education only; nothing here is medical advice.

## Design
- **Bank**: `server/data/bookExams/chNN.json` (server only, never under `public/`; listed in `vercel.json` `includeFiles`). Schema and scoring rules: `shared/bookExams.ts`. Each chapter has a topic **checklist** (everything the book teaches there that an exam must cover), 18-27 multiple-choice questions and 3 short essays.
- **Multiple choice**: 4 options, one best answer, level (`recall`, `image`, `next`, `hard`), an explanation (why right, why the others are wrong), a `book` pointer ("Chapter N, section") and, sometimes, a `lesson` (a real case that shows it, opened from the result). The server shuffles the options for each attempt and keeps the order.
- **Essays**: 5-6 real images from the case packs (series thumbnails; neutral captions, no diagnosis, no patient identity), a written answer of 2-4 sentences, scored against a **rubric** (3-8 ideas, each a list of words or phrases matched from a word start). No outside service is called. After finishing, the learner sees the rubric, the model answer and can tick the ideas the answer really contains (the keyword match is only a first estimate); the essay's points follow the ticks. An answer under 12 words earns nothing.
- **Marking is on the server**: `POST /api/book-exams/start` returns questions without answers, explanations or rubrics; `/finish` marks and returns the full result; `/review` saves the learner's own rubric ticks; `GET /api/book-exams` lists chapters with best/last; `GET /api/book-exams/attempt?id=` returns an open exam (to carry on) or a finished result.
- **Storage** (Upstash, few commands): `rvm:bookexam:a:<attempt>` (90 days: option order, the learner's written essays and results, for that learner only) and `rvm:bookexam:u:<user>` (best, last, tries per chapter). Answers in progress wait in the browser (localStorage) until the exam is finished.
- **Scoring**: 1 point per multiple-choice question; each essay is worth its rubric points (6-9). Pass mark 70%. Best and last result are kept per chapter.
- **Access**: an active membership **and** the book (`servesBook(email)`: SSST student emails and named accounts, the same rule that serves the PDF, `server/book.ts`). The account's `book` flag (from `/api/session`) lets the page say so without a request; the server checks again (402 no membership, 403 no book). A reader who only opened their own PDF on a device cannot be verified, so is not covered.
- **Images** are served by `proxy.ts` to members, like every pack file. The tests check each image's case, thumbnail and file.

## Checklist coverage
Each checklist item of each chapter, with the questions that cover it (`m` = multiple choice, `e` = essay). `tests/bookExams.test.ts` fails if an item is not covered.


### Chapter 1: Recognizing Anything: Past, Present, and Future
22 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 32 checklist items

- Roentgen discovered x-rays in 1895 (fluorescent screen glowed near a light-proof cathode ray tube); named 'x' for the unknown; first Nobel Prize in Physics 1901 (m01)
- Conventional radiograph (plain film) = ionizing radiation without added contrast; needs x-ray source, a way to record the image, a way to process it (m02, e01)
- Film era to digital: 'wet reading' now 'stat interpretation'; digital images stored and shared on a PACS (Picture Archiving, Communications and Storage system) (m02)
- Conventional radiography advantages (quick, inexpensive, portable, most widely obtained) and common uses (chest film, abdomen, first skeletal images for fracture or arthritis) (m03)
- Conventional radiography disadvantages: limited range of densities and ionizing radiation (only medically necessary studies; avoid in pregnancy) (m03)
- Five basic densities from whitest to blackest: metal, calcium (bone), fluid or soft tissue, fat, air (m04, e03)
- Blood (fluid) and muscle (soft tissue) have the same density on a radiograph, so blood inside the heart cannot be seen (Case Quiz 1) (m05, e03)
- CT: gantry with rotating x-ray beam and detectors plus computer; introduced in the 1970s; helical (spiral) acquisition gives a 3D dataset (m06, e01)
- CT numbers in Hounsfield units: pixels in a matrix, water = 0, scale +1000 to -1000, Hounsfield and Cormack (Nobel Prize 1979) (m06, e02)
- Hounsfield values of tissues: air -1000, fat about -40 to -120, water 0, soft tissue about +20 to +100, bone about +400 to +600, metal +1000 or more (m07, e02)
- Denser = more x-rays absorbed = higher CT number = increased attenuation = whiter (opaque on radiographs); less dense = low CT number = decreased attenuation = blacker (lucent) (m08, e02, e03)
- CT window = range of Hounsfield numbers displayed over the gray scale; lung, mediastinal and bone windows; post-processing without rescanning or re-exposing the patient (m09, e02)
- Imaging planes: axial (transverse), coronal, sagittal (midsagittal, parasagittal); volumetric data allow any plane and 3D rendering; right on viewer's left (e02)
- Multislice CT: head to toe in under 10 seconds; virtual colonoscopy and bronchoscopy, calcium scoring, CT coronary angiography; studies read by scrolling on workstations (m10)
- CT advantages (wider gray scale, less overlap, safe with a pacemaker) and disadvantages (ionizing radiation, expensive, space and computing, not portable) (m11)
- Ultrasound: acoustic energy above human hearing, transducer produces and records, no ionizing radiation, static or cine images (m12, e01)
- Ultrasound advantages: inexpensive, widely available, portable to handheld; safe in pregnancy, children and women of child-bearing age (m12, e01)
- Ultrasound disadvantages: cannot penetrate bone, large gas-filled structures disrupt it, difficult in obese patients, operator-dependent (m13)
- Ultrasound uses: first choice for female pelvis and children, cystic versus solid, vascular, fetus and placenta, guidance for aspiration and biopsy; fluid-filled cysts have no echoes (m13)
- MRI principle: hydrogen atoms (mostly in water) in a very strong magnetic field and radio-frequency pulses, computer reconstruction (m14, e01)
- MRI advantages: no ionizing radiation, best soft-tissue contrast, equal resolution in any plane, flow and diffusion (stroke); calcium gives no signal so contents of posterior fossa and spine can be imaged; neuroimaging, muscles, tendons, ligaments (m14, e01)
- Gadolinium: intravenous chelated rare-earth metal for lesion detection (tumor, abscess, metastasis) and MR angiography (m15)
- MRI disadvantages and safety: less available, expensive, needs site construction, high running cost; magnetic field hazards (pacemakers, ferromagnetic projectiles such as oxygen tanks), radio-frequency side effects, contrast reactions (m11, m15)
- Fluoroscopy: real-time ionizing imaging of motion on a tilting table; spot films and overhead films in barium studies; interventional use with iodinated contrast in vessels and ducts (m16, e01)
- Fluoroscopy advantages (mobile units, device placement, foreign body removal, biopsy guidance) and dose (can be much higher than a plain film, so use the shortest fluoroscopy time) (m16)
- Nuclear medicine principle: radioisotope (radionuclide, tracer) + carrier = radiopharmaceutical; targets (thyroid iodine, brain glucose, bone phosphates, lung capillaries); gamma camera; technetium-99m is the commonest (m17)
- SPECT (gamma camera rotating around the patient, 3D reconstruction) and PET (positron emitter, FDG as glucose analog, metabolism; about 90% oncologic) (m18)
- Nuclear medicine uses: hidden metastases and recurrence, heart and lung function, bone (metastases, fracture, infection), thyroid treatment (m18)
- Nuclear medicine radiation: generally lower dose than CT or fluoroscopy, highest for cardiac and PET studies; patient is briefly a source of exposure to others (time, distance, shielding) (m19)
- Nuclear medicine image orientation: anterior and posterior views; on posterior views the patient's right is on the viewer's right; check labels or the marker dot (m20)
- Artificial intelligence: intelligence demonstrated by a machine; supervised learning versus deep learning (neural networks); aids workflow, quantitation and diagnosis (e.g. ground glass versus consolidation in COVID-19) (m21)
- Book conventions: bold for key points, new terms first in bold italic, icons for pitfalls, important points and take-home points, Case Quiz answer at the end of the chapter (m22)

### Chapter 2: Recognizing Normal Pulmonary Anatomy
22 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 27 checklist items

- Purpose: learn normal anatomy and technical adequacy first so that abnormal can be told from normal; you only see what you look for and only look for what you know (m22)
- Lung markings: virtually all branching white lines are blood vessels, branching and tapering gradually from hila to periphery; arteries and veins cannot be told apart; bronchi are normally invisible (thin-walled, air-filled, surrounded by air) (m01)
- Pleura: outer parietal and inner visceral layer with pleural space between; visceral pleura adheres to lung and forms the major and minor fissures; a few mL of fluid but no air; invisible except as pencil-thin fissure lines (m02)
- Normal vasculature upright: vessels at the bases larger in size (not number) than at the apices because of gravity; all taper gradually (m03, e03)
- Frontal landmarks: left hilum slightly higher than right; sharp acute costophrenic angles; minor fissure on the right only; hemidiaphragms; spine faintly seen through the heart (m04, e03)
- Five technical parameters: penetration, inspiration, rotation, magnification, angulation (m06, e01)
- Penetration: spine and left hemidiaphragm seen through the heart; underpenetrated (too light) obscures left base and accentuates markings; overpenetrated (too dark) mimics emphysema or pneumothorax (m05, e01)
- Inspiration: at least 8 to 9 (almost 10) posterior ribs above the diaphragm; poor inspiration crowds basal markings (mimics aspiration or pneumonia), makes the heart look larger; lateral view helps (m07, e01)
- Rotation: spinous process midway between the medial ends (heads) of the clavicles; rotation distorts heart, hila and diaphragm; Case Quiz 2 (rotated patient, heart seems on the wrong side) (m08, e01)
- Magnification and angulation: AP (portable) films magnify the heart versus PA; medial clavicle should overlie the anterior first rib; apical lordotic view (semi-recumbent portable) puts clavicles above the first ribs, distorts the heart, obscures left hemidiaphragm (m09, e01)
- Anterior versus posterior ribs: posterior ribs more distinct and horizontal, attach to vertebrae; anterior ribs less distinct, angle downward, attach by cartilage (visible only if calcified) (m10)
- Lateral view: standard two-view study = upright frontal plus upright left lateral (left side against the detector); reasons (localise, confirm, reveal disease not seen on frontal) (m11, m12, e03)
- Normal lateral chest: clear retrosternal space, no discrete hilar shadow, vertebral bodies of equal height with parallel end plates, sharp posterior costophrenic angles, spine gets blacker toward the diaphragm; spine sign (lower lobe airspace disease makes it whiter) (m12)
- Five key areas on the lateral: retrosternal clear space, hilar region, fissures, thoracic spine, diaphragm and costophrenic sulci (m13)
- Retrosternal clear space fills in with an anterior mediastinal mass (adenopathy such as lymphoma is the commonest; thymoma, teratoma, substernal thyroid); pitfall: arms (identify the humeri) (m13)
- Hilar region: mostly pulmonary arteries; no discrete mass should show on the lateral; lobulated hilar masses = adenopathy (e.g. sarcoidosis) or tumor; lateral helps when both hila are enlarged (m14)
- Fissures: major and minor both visible on lateral as fine lines (1 to 2 mm); major from about T5 to anterior diaphragm 2 cm behind sternum; minor at the 4th anterior rib level, right only, the only fissure usually seen on the frontal; thickening by fluid (with Kerley B lines, effusions) versus fibrosis (no other fluid signs) (m15)
- Thoracic spine on the lateral: rectangular bodies, parallel end plates, disk spaces the same or taller going down; osteoporotic compression fracture (loss of height, superior endplate first); degenerative disk disease (narrowing, osteophytes); clue to systemic disorders (m16)
- Diaphragm: only the upper border is seen; right hemidiaphragm slightly higher and seen in full on the lateral; left silhouetted anteriorly by the heart; stomach or splenic flexure gas under the left, liver under the right (m17, e03)
- Costophrenic sulci: lowest point of the pleural space when upright; sharp and acute; effusion blunts them; about 75 mL blunts the posterior angle on the lateral, 250 to 300 mL the lateral angle on the frontal (m18, e03)
- CT viewing: patient's right on the viewer's left; supine patient has anterior at the top of the image (e02)
- CT windows: lung, mediastinal and bone windows are manipulations of the same data (lungs black on mediastinal window, mediastinum white on lung window); no rescanning (e02)
- CT of lungs: vessels seen from hilum to pleura; bronchus normally smaller than its accompanying artery (reversed in bronchiectasis); trachea oval, about 2 cm; MIP post-processing (e02)
- Aortopulmonary window: space below the aortic arch and above the pulmonary artery (bounded by arch, left pulmonary artery, trachea, left lung); favorite site for enlarged lymph nodes; not the congenital lesion of the same name (m19, e02)
- Carina and bronchi on CT: trachea bifurcates at or just below the AP window; right main bronchus circular then tubular at the right upper lobe bronchus; bronchus intermedius; only aerated lung posterior to it (soft tissue = tumor or adenopathy); left main bronchus circular (m20, e02)
- Fissures on CT: thin white lines or avascular bands up to 2 cm; major separates lower from upper lobe (left) and lower from upper and middle lobes (right); minor separates right upper and middle lobes and lies in the axial plane (seen on sagittal and coronal, not axial); lingula = middle lobe analog (m21, e02)
- Approach to a chest film: no single best system as long as everything on all views is examined (including the lateral); gestalt of experts; checklist technical adequacy, soft tissues, bones, heart, lungs, diaphragm (m22)

### Chapter 3: Recognizing Normal Cardiac Anatomy
22 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 25 checklist items

- Cardiothoracic ratio: widest transverse heart diameter over widest internal rib-cage diameter at the diaphragm; normally under 50% in adults at full inspiration (m01, e03)
- Outflow obstruction (aortic stenosis, coarctation, pulmonic stenosis, systemic hypertension): ventricle hypertrophies first, lumen shrinks, heart enlarges only when it decompensates (m02, e03)
- Cardiomegaly on a radiograph = enlarged silhouette from ventricular enlargement, not isolated atrial enlargement (e.g. early mitral stenosis) (m03, e03)
- Volume overload enlarges chambers more than pressure overload: heart bigger in aortic regurgitation than stenosis; left atrium bigger in mitral regurgitation than stenosis (m04, e03)
- Normal frontal contours: right = ascending aorta then right atrium; left from the top = aortic knob, main pulmonary artery, left atrial appendage indentation, left ventricle; descending aorta along the spine (m05, e03)
- Contour rules: ascending aorta not beyond right heart border; knob under 35 mm and pushes trachea slightly right; pulmonary artery segment flat or concave (convex in young women); enlarged LA fills the concavity or makes a double density; LV posterior and RV anterior; tortuous descending aorta swings left (m06, m07, m08)
- Aorta in aortic stenosis (convex ascending aorta from post-stenotic dilatation, normal knob) versus systemic hypertension (ascending and descending aorta both too far out, knob enlarged); Case Quiz 3 (m07)
- Cardiac CT technique: fast multislice, intravenous iodinated contrast, ECG gating (images in the least-motion phase, usually diastole); ECG gating also in cardiac MRI (m09)
- Six key CT levels top to bottom: five-vessel, aortic arch, aortopulmonary window, main pulmonary artery, high cardiac, low cardiac; right and left mean the patient's (m10, e01)
- Five-vessel level: trachea (black, oval, about 2 cm), esophagus behind it, venous structures more anterior than arterial, brachiocephalic veins behind the sternum, arteries right to left innominate, left common carotid, left subclavian (m10)
- Aortic arch level: inverted U (comma-shaped if the very top is cut); SVC to the right of the trachea receives the azygos vein (m10)
- Aortopulmonary window level: ascending and descending aorta, SVC, top of left pulmonary artery; AP window (lymph nodes); carina at or just below; ascending aorta 2.5 to 3.5 cm, descending 2 to 3 cm (m11)
- Main pulmonary artery level: main, right and left pulmonary arteries (LPA higher, continuing the main PA; RPA at 90 degrees passing behind the ascending aorta; LPA anterior to descending aorta), main bronchi, bronchus intermedius with only lung behind (m12, e01)
- High cardiac level: left atrium (posterior and central, pulmonary veins entering), right atrium (right border), aortic root, right ventricular outflow tract; PALS mnemonic (pulmonic valve anterior, lateral, superior to aortic valve) (m13, e01)
- Low cardiac level: RA, RV (anterior behind sternum, more trabeculated, thinner wall), LV (left border, smoother, thicker wall), interventricular septum; normal pericardium about 2 mm outlined by fat (m14, e01)
- Uses of cardiac CT: coronary arteries, masses, aorta including dissection, pericardial disease (also valves, wall motion, ejection fraction, perfusion); CCTA for acute or chronic chest pain; dose reduced (m15)
- Coronary calcium scoring: unenhanced CT, amount of calcium relates to atherosclerosis; mainly risk assessment in asymptomatic patients; zero has high negative predictive value; higher score = higher risk (100 to 300 mild to moderate, over 300 greater) (m16)
- Left coronary artery: from the left coronary cusp, divides almost at once into LAD (anterior interventricular groove to apex; diagonal and septal branches) and circumflex (between LA and LV; obtuse marginals to lateral LV wall) (m17)
- Right coronary artery: from the right aortic sinus, between RA and RV; conus branch (RVOT), sinus node artery, acute marginal (lateral RV wall), AV node branch, posterior descending artery (inferior LV wall and inferior septum) (m18)
- Coronary dominance set by the artery giving the PDA: right (most people), left (circumflex, about 10%, more non-fatal MI and mortality), co-dominant (m19)
- Triple rule-out emergent CT for acute chest pain: coronary disease, aortic dissection and pulmonary embolism together (m15)
- Cardiac MRI: anatomy and function with ECG gating and breath-holds; infarct scar, perfusion, defects, masses, valves, chambers; with or without gadolinium; helpful in children with congenital heart disease when echocardiography is inconclusive (m20, e02)
- Cardiac MRI views: axial, sagittal, coronal plus horizontal long-axis (four-chamber), vertical long-axis (two-chamber), short-axis, three-chamber (m21, e02)
- Use of each view: four-chamber for septal and lateral LV walls, apex, RV free wall, chamber size, mitral and tricuspid valves; vertical long-axis for anterior and inferior walls and apex; short-axis for volumes, mass, stroke volume, ejection fraction; three-chamber for mitral and aortic valves, LA size, LV walls (m21, e02)
- Bright blood (gradient echo; function) versus black blood (spin echo; morphology) on cardiac MRI (m22, e02)

### Chapter 4: Recognizing Airspace Versus Interstitial Lung Disease
22 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 33 checklist items

- Parenchymal disease is divided (arbitrarily, by pathology and imaging pattern) into airspace (alveolar) and interstitial (infiltrative); recognising the pattern narrows the differential; many diseases show both (m01, e01)
- Airspace disease: fluffy, cloud-like, hazy opacities (m02, e01)
- Airspace disease: indistinct margins and confluent (blending) opacities (m02, e01)
- Airspace disease may be diffuse (pulmonary edema) or localised (segmental or lobar pneumonia) (m06, e03)
- Air bronchogram: what it is, and why bronchi are normally invisible (thin walls, air inside, air around) (m03, m07, e01)
- An air bronchogram is almost always airspace disease; what fills airspaces (fluid, blood, gastric juice, exudate, water) (m03, e01)
- Silhouette sign: same-density structures in contact lose their edge; it localises disease anywhere in the body (m04)
- Silhouette sign in right middle lobe pneumonia: right heart border lost, hemidiaphragm still seen, minor fissure above (m04)
- Box 4.1 airspace diseases: acute (pneumonia, alveolar edema, hemorrhage, aspiration, near-drowning) and chronic (adenocarcinoma, alveolar proteinosis, sarcoidosis, lymphoma) (m12)
- Pneumonia: about 90% of community-acquired lobar/segmental cases are Streptococcus pneumoniae; patchy, segmental or lobar; air bronchograms possible; clears in under 10 days (pneumococcal may clear within 48 hours) (m05)
- Alveolar edema: bilateral perihilar bat-wing/angel-wing pattern, may be asymmetric, effusions and thickened fissures, usually no air bronchograms, clears in under 48 hours (m06, m07, m13, e03)
- Aspiration: dependent segments (lower lobes, posterior upper lobes), right lower lobe more than left; bland juice or water clears in 24 to 48 hours, infected aspiration takes weeks (m08, m13)
- The interstitium (connective tissue, lymphatics, vessels, bronchi) produces discrete "particles" of disease (m09, e01)
- Box 4.3 interstitial disease: packets with sharp margins separated by aerated lung, focal or diffuse, usually no air bronchograms (m09, e01)
- Three interstitial patterns: reticular (lines), nodular (dots), reticulonodular (both, the most frequent) (m09)
- Pitfall: heavy interstitial disease can overlap and mimic airspace disease on radiographs; look at the periphery of the shadow; CT characterises it better (m10, e01)
- Box 4.1 interstitial diseases: reticular (interstitial edema, interstitial pneumonia, scleroderma, sarcoid) and nodular (bronchogenic carcinoma, metastases, silicosis, miliary TB, sarcoid) (m12, e02)
- Pulmonary interstitial edema: mechanisms (capillary pressure, permeability, decreased resorption); precursor of alveolar edema; four key signs (fissure fluid, peribronchial cuffing, effusions, Kerley B lines); few rales; clears in under 48 hours (m11, m13, m17, e03)
- Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP/IPF): older men, smoking, reflux; fine basal reticular pattern then honeycombing and volume loss; CT: honeycombing, subpleural reticulation, traction bronchiectasis; end stage (m14)
- Nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP): connective tissue disease such as scleroderma, better prognosis than UIP; CT basal ground-glass opacity, subpleural sparing, traction bronchiectasis; meaning of ground-glass (m15)
- Bronchogenic carcinoma: four cell types; adenocarcinoma as a solitary peripheral nodule; nodules and masses are more discrete than airspace disease; CT shows spiculation (e02)
- Hematogenous metastases: two or more nodules, cannonball when large; classic primaries; multiple lung nodules = metastases until proven otherwise (m16, e02)
- Lymphangitic spread: resembles interstitial edema but localised to a segment, lobe or one lung; Kerley lines, fissure fluid, effusions; classic primaries (breast, lung, stomach, pancreas) (m17, e02)
- Direct extension: the least common route (pleura is resistant); subpleural mass with rib destruction (e02)
- Sarcoidosis: bilateral hilar and right paratracheal adenopathy; about half have lung disease, reticulonodular; stages I to IV; most parenchymal disease resolves (m18)
- Tuberculosis can show airspace and interstitial patterns; about a third of the world infected, about 1 in 10 become active (m19)
- Primary TB: few symptoms; upper lobes slightly more than lower; lobar pneumonia, hilar/mediastinal adenopathy alone (children), large silent effusions (adults); cavitation rare (m19)
- Postprimary TB: reactivation; apical and posterior upper-lobe segments and superior segments of lower lobes; caseous necrosis and tubercle; heals with fibrosis and contraction (m20)
- Postprimary TB patterns: bilateral upper-lobe thin-walled cavities with no air-fluid level; pneumonia; transbronchial spread (including the lingula) (m20)
- TB sequelae: bronchiectasis (usually asymptomatic), bronchostenosis and middle lobe syndrome, tuberculoma with satellite lesions, effusion in postprimary TB = empyema with a graver prognosis (m21)
- Miliary TB: insidious (fever, chills, night sweats); weeks until visible; nodules 1 mm growing to 2-3 mm; clears rapidly with treatment; seldom calcifies; other miliary causes (histoplasmosis, sarcoid, silicosis) (m22)
- Healed varicella pneumonia: innumerable small calcified granulomas seen as discrete nodules (m22)
- Case quiz: fluffy, confluent density with air bronchograms and fever = airspace disease, most likely pneumonia (e01)

### Chapter 5: Recognizing the Causes of an Opacified Hemithorax
18 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 20 checklist items

- Four causes of an opacified hemithorax: atelectasis of the entire lung, a very large pleural effusion, pneumonia of the entire lung, and (less common) pneumonectomy (m01, e01)
- Entire-lung atelectasis: complete obstruction of the right or left main bronchus; air beyond it is absorbed into the blood and the lung loses volume (m02, e02)
- Causes of entire-lung atelectasis: obstructing neoplasm in adults, asthma mucous plugs in younger people, aspirated foreign body (peanuts in children), mucous plugs in critically ill patients (m02, e02)
- In obstructive atelectasis the visceral and parietal pleura do not separate, so atelectasis and pneumothorax look completely different (m03, e02)
- Mobile structures (heart, trachea, hemidiaphragms) shift toward the opacified side in atelectasis (toward the volume loss); a shift can be a pull or a push (m04, e01, e02)
- Table 5.2, right-sided atelectasis: heart moves right (left heart border near the spine), trachea moves right, right hemidiaphragm rises and may disappear (silhouette sign) (m05, e02)
- Table 5.2, left-sided atelectasis: heart moves left (right heart border overlaps the spine), trachea moves left, left hemidiaphragm rises and may disappear; the stomach bubble is elevated (m06, m18, e02)
- Table 5.1: pneumothorax is black/lucent with never a shift toward it; obstructive atelectasis is whiter with almost always a shift toward it (m07, e02)
- In whole-lung atelectasis the opposite lung over-expands and crosses the midline (m08, e02)
- Massive pleural effusion: blood, exudate or transudate; acts like a mass; the compressed lung beneath is usually not visible on radiographs (m09, e01)
- Causes of massive effusion: malignancy (bronchogenic carcinoma, pleural metastases), trauma (hemothorax), tuberculosis (large silent effusions); heart-failure effusions are usually bilateral, asymmetric and rarely fill a hemithorax (m10)
- A large effusion pushes the heart and trachea away from the opacified side; Table 5.3 (right effusion: heart to the left, apex near the left lateral chest wall; hemidiaphragm disappears) (m11, m12, e01)
- Pitfall: a large malignant effusion can be balanced by obstructive atelectasis, giving no shift (suggests central bronchogenic carcinoma, CT shows it) (m13, m14, e01)
- An adult with an opacified hemithorax, no air bronchograms and little or no shift: suspect an obstructing bronchogenic carcinoma, perhaps with pleural metastases (m14, e01)
- Pneumonia of the entire lung: exudate fills the airspaces; no pull and no push, so no shift of heart or trachea; air bronchograms may be present (Table 5.4) (m15, e01, e03)
- Pneumonectomy: removal of a lung; 5th or 6th rib is removed; metallic surgical clips at the hilum (m16, e03)
- Postpneumonectomy course: air for about 24 hours, hemithorax fills with fluid over about 2 weeks, completely opaque by about 4 months; fibrosis makes it permanent with shift toward the operated side (m17, e03)
- Pitfall: postpneumonectomy can look identical to atelectasis; look for the missing rib and hilar clips (m16, e03)
- Case quiz: opaque left hemithorax with trachea pulled to the left and an elevated stomach bubble = atelectasis from an obstructing bronchogenic carcinoma in the left main bronchus (m18, e02)
- The trachea, heart and hemidiaphragms are mobile structures that move if something pushes or pulls on them; the direction of shift is the key to the cause (m04, e01)

### Chapter 6: Recognizing Atelectasis
22 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 31 checklist items

- Definition: atelectasis is loss of volume of some or all of the lung, frequently (not always) with increased density (the lung is black because of its air) (m01, e01)
- Types (Table 6.1): obstructive (resorptive) versus nonobstructive (passive, adhesive, subsegmental, cicatrization) with their mechanisms (m02, e02)
- Obstructive atelectasis: air resorbed through the capillary bed beyond a blocked bronchus; pleural layers stay in contact so mobile structures shift toward it (m03, e02)
- Most common causes of obstructive atelectasis (Table 6.2): tumours (squamous cell, endobronchial metastases, carcinoid), mucous plugs, foreign body aspiration (peanuts, toys, traumatic intubation) (m04)
- Speed of collapse: about 18 to 24 hours for a whole lung on room air, under an hour on near-100% oxygen (m05, e03)
- Box 6.1 signs: displaced fissure toward the collapse, increased density, shift of mobile structures toward it, compensatory overinflation (m06, e01)
- Normal trachea (midline with slight rightward deviation at the aortic knob) and shift toward volume loss (m07, e01)
- Right heart border at least 1 cm to the right of the spine; heart shifted right brings the left heart border toward the spine, shifted left brings the right heart border over the spine (m07, e01)
- Hemidiaphragms: right normally higher than left by half an interspace (left higher in about 10%); lower lobe atelectasis silhouettes the hemidiaphragm; elevated stomach bubble shows a raised left hemidiaphragm (m07, e01)
- Compensatory overinflation of unaffected lobes or the opposite lung (crossing the midline; larger retrosternal clear space on the lateral view) (m08, e01)
- Passive (compressive) atelectasis: loss of pleural contact from effusion, pneumothorax or mass, or poor inspiration; usually re-expands when the cause is removed (m09, e02)
- Atelectasis and effusion in balance: opaque hemithorax, no air bronchograms, no shift; ominous, suspicious for central bronchogenic carcinoma with metastases (m10)
- Pitfall: poor inspiration (fewer than eight posterior ribs) causes basal passive atelectasis that mimics airspace disease; check the lateral view (m11)
- Review of Table 5.1: large pneumothorax (black, no shift toward) versus atelectasis of the whole lung (white, shift toward) (e02)
- Adhesive atelectasis: surfactant (made by type II pneumocytes) lowers surface tension; deactivation in ARDS (adults and neonates); implicated in subsegmental atelectasis (m02, e02)
- Subsegmental (discoid, plate-like, linear) atelectasis: least volume loss; splinting (postoperative, pleuritic pain); linear bands parallel to the diaphragm at the bases; no shift; clears with deep breathing (m12, e02)
- Pitfall: subsegmental atelectasis and chronic linear scarring look identical on a single film; atelectasis disappears in days, scarring remains (m12)
- Round atelectasis: periphery of the lung base; prior pleural disease (asbestos, TB) plus effusion; mass-like and can mimic tumour; CT shows a comet tail of bronchovascular markings to the hilum (m13, e02)
- Cicatrization atelectasis: fibrosis and scarring, most often TB, mainly upper lobes (upper-lobe reticular pattern, pleural thickening, hilar retraction); other causes (histoplasmosis, silicosis, scleroderma, late radiation, IPF) (m14, e02)
- Lobar collapse is fan-shaped (base on the pleura, apex at the hilum); other lobes hyperinflate and can limit the shift (m15, e01)
- Pitfall: the more atelectatic a lobe, the less visible it is, so worsening can be mistaken for improvement; check fissure/hemidiaphragm displacement or use CT (m15, e03)
- Right upper lobe: fan-shaped density, minor fissure displaced upward, trachea to the right, juxtaphrenic peak; S sign of Golden with a hilar mass; lateral wedge with major fissure forward (m16)
- Left upper lobe: luftsichel sign, trachea and heart to the left, raised left hemidiaphragm; lateral band-like density with the major fissure displaced anteriorly (m17)
- Lower lobes: fan-shaped triangle bounded by the displaced major fissure, medial hemidiaphragm silhouetted; lateral: major fissure displaced posteriorly, small triangle in the posterior costophrenic sulcus (m19)
- In critically ill patients atelectasis is commonest in the left lower lobe; check that the left hemidiaphragm is seen through the heart shadow (m19, e03)
- Right middle lobe: triangular density, minor fissure displaced downward, right heart border lost but hemidiaphragm visible; lateral triangle with base anterior and apex at the hilum (m18)
- Atelectasis of the right lung: opaque hemithorax, trachea toward the right, right hemidiaphragm silhouetted, left heart border displaced toward/over the spine (e01)
- Atelectasis of the left lung: opaque hemithorax, trachea to the left, right heart border over the spine, right lung overinflated across the midline in front of the aorta, only the right hemidiaphragm visible (m20, e01)
- Resolution: acute atelectasis can clear within hours, chronic takes longer; slowly clearing collapse shows patchy airspace disease with growing aerated zones (m21, e03)
- Case quiz: endotracheal tube too deep in the bronchus intermedius aerates only the right middle and lower lobes; right upper lobe and whole left lung collapse; minor fissure elevated; withdrawing the tube clears it (m22, e03)
- Take-home: the three commonest types are subsegmental, compressive (passive) and obstructive; round atelectasis is a passive type that does not re-expand when the effusion recedes (e02)

### Chapter 7: Recognizing a Pleural Effusion
22 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 27 checklist items

- Pleural anatomy: parietal pleura lines the thoracic cage, visceral pleura covers the lung and forms the fissures (major and minor on the right, only major on the left); the pleural space is a potential space with about 2 to 5 mL (m01)
- Normal physiology: several hundred mL produced and reabsorbed daily; formed mainly at the parietal pleura, resorbed at the visceral pleura and by lymphatics through the parietal pleura (m03)
- Modalities: radiography is the first step; CT and ultrasound both sensitive for small amounts; CT best for underlying disease or a fully opacified hemithorax; ultrasound helps guide removal of fluid; appearances are fundamentally similar (m02)
- Causes: fluid forms faster than it is cleared; increased formation (hydrostatic pressure as in left-heart failure, low colloid osmotic pressure as in hypoproteinemia, capillary permeability as in pneumonia or hypersensitivity) (m04, e02)
- Causes: decreased resorption (lymphatic block, lymphangitic carcinomatosis, raised venous pressure; lowered pleural pressure from atelectasis due to bronchial obstruction) and transport of peritoneal fluid (ascites) (m04, e02)
- Transudate vs exudate by protein, specific gravity and LDH; transudates usually bilateral (congestive heart failure the commonest, hypoalbuminemia, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) (m05, e02)
- Exudates usually unilateral (inflammation, connective tissue disease, malignancy the commonest); empyema = exudate with pus; hemothorax = fluid hematocrit above 50% of blood; chylothorax = raised triglycerides or cholesterol (m06, e02)
- Bilateral effusions: heart failure gives about equal amounts (sometimes slightly more on the right), a marked difference suggests parapneumonic effusion or malignancy on the larger side; lupus is bilateral, and when unilateral usually left (m07, e02)
- Either side but usually unilateral: tuberculosis and other infectious exudates, pulmonary thromboembolism, trauma (e02)
- Usually left-sided only: pancreatitis, distal thoracic duct obstruction, Dressler syndrome (m08, m09, e02)
- Mostly right-sided: liver or ovarian disease (Meigs syndrome), rheumatoid arthritis (can stay unchanged for years), proximal thoracic duct obstruction (m08, e02)
- Dressler syndrome: 2 to 3 weeks after transmural infarct or cardiac surgery; fever, chest pain, left effusion, pericardial effusion, patchy left-base airspace disease; responds to aspirin or steroids (m09)
- Appearance depends on patient position, gravity, amount of fluid and elastic recoil of the lung; descriptions assume the upright patient (m10, e01)
- Subpulmonic effusion: where almost all effusions first collect; looks like an altered hemidiaphragm contour (really the fluid-lung interface); usually free-flowing, subpulmonic does not mean loculated (m11, e03)
- Right subpulmonic effusion: apparent hemidiaphragm peaks too far laterally, posterior arc then flat anterior drop at the major fissure; harder to see because liver and fluid have the same density (m12)
- Left subpulmonic effusion: more than about 1 cm between stomach bubble and apparent hemidiaphragm, peak displaced laterally, flat anterior contour on the lateral view (m13)
- Blunting thresholds: about 75 mL blunts the posterior costophrenic sulcus (lateral view); about 300 mL (200 to 300) blunts the lateral sulcus (frontal view) (m14, e01)
- Pitfall: pleural scarring also blunts the angle, with a ski-slope rather than meniscoid shape, and does not move with position (m15, e03)
- Meniscus sign: elastic recoil makes fluid higher laterally than medially on the frontal view (U-shaped on the lateral); a meniscoid density strongly suggests effusion (m10, e01)
- Positioning: upright fluid falls to the base; supine fluid layers posteriorly as a homogeneous haze over the hemithorax; change in appearance may be position not volume, so keep portable films in the same position (m16, e03)
- Decubitus view: patient lies on a side, horizontal beam; free fluid forms a band along the dependent side; now used infrequently (m17)
- Opacified hemithorax (about 2 L): passive atelectasis, mass effect pushes heart and trachea away from the side; CT shows the lung and pleura hidden behind the fluid (e.g. pleural metastases) (m18, e01)
- Loculated effusion: adhesions (old infection, hemothorax) fix the fluid; unusual shape or location, defies gravity; hard to drain with a single tube (m19, e03)
- Fissural pseudotumor: lenticular, pointed ends (lemon), mostly minor fissure (75%), transudate in heart failure, not free-flowing, vanishes with treatment and recurs in the same place (m20, e03)
- Laminar effusion: thin band along the lateral chest wall, costophrenic angle stays acute; raised left atrial pressure (heart failure or lymphangitic carcinomatosis); not free-flowing (m21, e03)
- Hydropneumothorax: air plus fluid; straight air-fluid level with horizontal beam instead of a meniscus; causes trauma, surgery, thoracentesis, bronchopleural fistula (m22, e03)
- Radiography cannot tell blood from other fluid (hemopneumothorax in the case quiz); CT may be needed to tell hydropneumothorax from a large lung abscess (m22)

### Chapter 8: Recognizing Pneumonia
22 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 28 checklist items

- Definition: pneumonia is consolidation of the lung produced by inflammatory exudate, usually from an infectious agent; most are airspace disease (lobar or segmental), others interstitial, some both (m01)
- Spread: most organisms reach the lungs through the tracheobronchial tree (inhalation or aspiration), some by the bloodstream, few by direct extension (m02)
- The organism cannot be identified with certainty from the radiograph alone, though some patterns are suggestive; "infiltrate" is not a synonym for pneumonia (m02, m03, e01)
- Table 8.1 patterns: upper lobe cavitary pneumonia spreading to the opposite lower lobe (TB), perihilar interstitial or airspace disease (Pneumocystis), spherical mass in a thin-walled upper lobe cavity (aspergilloma), bilateral peripheral lower lobe ground-glass (SARS-CoV-2) (m03)
- General signs: pneumonia is denser than normal lung, homogeneous, fluffy indistinct margins except where it abuts a pleural surface (sharp); air bronchograms (more visible centrally, not specific for pneumonia) (m04, e01)
- Interstitial pneumonia gives reticular prominence; pneumonias may be associated with atelectasis (Box 8.1 key signs) (m04, e01)
- Patterns (lobar, segmental, interstitial, round, cavitary) describe distribution and are not diagnostic of pneumonia (m22, e01)
- Lobar pneumonia: pneumococcus is the prototype; fills most or all of a lobe; sharp margin at a fissure; silhouette sign against heart, aorta or diaphragm; central air bronchograms (m05, e01)
- Segmental pneumonia (bronchopneumonia): Staphylococcus aureus prototype, also Pseudomonas and gram-negatives; spreads centrifugally to many foci; all margins indistinct; no air bronchograms because bronchi are filled; volume loss; staph in children, hospitalized, debilitated, after influenza (m06, e01)
- Interstitial pneumonia: prototypes viral, Mycoplasma and Pneumocystis; involves airway walls and septa; fine reticular pattern early, later airspace disease that hides the interstitial nature (m07, e01)
- Pneumocystis pneumonia: commonest clinically recognized infection in AIDS; perihilar reticular or edema-like airspace disease; usually no effusions and no hilar adenopathy; CD4 count under 200 (m07)
- Round pneumonia: mostly children, posterior lower lobes, Haemophilus, Streptococcus, Pneumococcus; mimics a mass but childhood tumors are uncommon and symptoms of infection are present (m08)
- Cavitary pneumonia: tuberculosis is the prototype; postprimary TB has upper lobe, bilateral, thin-walled cavities with smooth inner margin and no air-fluid level; cavitation is rare in primary TB; transbronchial spread to the opposite lower lobe or another lobe suggests TB (m09)
- Other cavitating pneumonias: staphylococcal (thin-walled pneumatoceles), streptococcal, Klebsiella, coccidiomycosis (m10)
- Aspiration: predisposing causes (neurologic disease, altered mental status, reflux, head and neck surgery); affects the most dependent lung (lower lobes upright; superior segments of lower lobes and posterior segments of upper lobes supine) (m11)
- Bland aspiration (neutralized juice or water) is not a pneumonia and clears in a day or two; infected aspirate (anaerobes such as Bacteroides, in the debilitated, elderly or immunocompromised) gives lower lobe disease that often cavitates and takes months (m12)
- Aspiration of unneutralized stomach acid (Mendelson syndrome): chemical pneumonitis appearing within hours, dependent airspace disease or diffuse edema, clears over days or longer, prone to secondary infection (m12)
- COVID-19 imaging: SARS-CoV-2; radiograph shows patchy or diffuse airspace disease, effusions uncommon; CT shows bilateral, posterior, lower lobe ground-glass opacities, nonspecific, usually without contrast (m13, e03)
- Ground-glass opacity: increased attenuation with preserved bronchial and vascular markings (nonspecific); crazy-paving: ground-glass with superimposed septal thickening (m14, e03)
- Imaging can be normal early or in asymptomatic infection (up to 18%; under 10% normal CT once symptomatic); imaging is advised for clinical worsening or complications (ARDS, pulmonary embolism, superimposed pneumonia, heart failure) (m15, e03)
- Localizing: location gives clues (upper lobes think TB, lower lobes think recurrent aspiration); use two orthogonal views (frontal and lateral); CT adds localization and associated effusions or small cavities (m16, e02)
- Silhouette sign: two objects of the same radiographic density in contact lose their shared edge (m05, m17, e02)
- Frontal-film localization on the right: right upper lobe hides the ascending aorta with a sharp minor-fissure margin; right middle lobe hides the right heart border; right lower lobe hides the right hemidiaphragm but spares the heart border (m17, e02)
- Frontal-film localization on the left: left upper lobe hides the aortic knob; lingula hides the left heart border but spares the left hemidiaphragm; left lower lobe hides the left hemidiaphragm but spares the left heart border (m17, e02)
- Spine sign: on the lateral view the spine should get blacker toward the diaphragm; posterior lower lobe disease keeps it white; may be the only sign of lower lobe pneumonia (m18, e02)
- Resolution: 2 to 3 days if the organism is sensitive (especially pneumococcus); usually resolves from within (vacuolizes), patchily over days or weeks (m19)
- A pneumonia that does not resolve in several weeks: consider an obstructing lesion such as a neoplasm; CT can show it (m20)
- Case quiz: aspirated barium during a swallowing study is inert and dense, gravitates to lower lobe bronchi, does not cause pneumonia and is reabsorbed (m21)

### Chapter 9: Recognizing the Correct Placement of Lines and Tubes and Their Potential Complications: Critical Care Radiology
27 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 42 checklist items

- Purpose: portable ICU films check device position and cardiopulmonary status; a film is taken after insertion to check position and rule out unintended consequences (e01)
- For each device the book asks: why it is used, where it belongs, how it can be malpositioned and what complications follow (e01)
- Box 9.1 warning signs after a tube/line entering the thorax: new or larger pleural effusion, pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, widened mediastinum; after an abdominal device: pneumoperitoneum (m11, e01, e02)
- Endotracheal tube (ETT) uses: assist ventilation, airway control, prevent gastric distension, suctioning, medication (m01)
- ETT appearance: wide-bore (about 1 cm), radiopaque marker stripe, no side-holes, diagonal tip, usually a cuff (m01)
- ETT position: head neutral (mandible at C5-C6), tip 3 to 5 cm above the carina, about half way between the medial clavicles and the carina (m01, m04, e01)
- ETT size and cuff: tube 1/2 to 2/3 of tracheal width; cuff fills but must not distend the trachea (overinflation: necrosis, tracheal stenosis) (m05)
- Finding the carina: follow the main bronchi back to where they meet; projects over T5-T7 in 95% of people (m01)
- Neck movement: flexion moves the ETT tip down about 2 cm, extension up about 2 cm (reason for the 3-5 cm margin); "tip of the hose follows the tip of the nose" (m02)
- Most common ETT malposition: right main bronchus/bronchus intermedius (shallower angle, wider); atelectasis (left lung, nonaerated RUL), right tension pneumothorax (m03)
- ETT in the esophagus gives a dilated stomach (m05)
- ETT too high (larynx/pharynx): vocal cord damage by the cuff, aspiration (m05)
- Tracheostomy tube uses: obstruction at or above the larynx, long-term intubation (>21 days), sleep apnea, paralysis of swallowing/respiratory muscles (m06)
- Tracheostomy tube position: tip half way between stoma and carina, width about 2/3 of trachea, not moved by head flexion/extension; check for pneumomediastinum, pneumothorax, subcutaneous emphysema after insertion (m06)
- Tracheostomy late complication: tracheal stenosis, most common late, most often at the stoma (also at cuff or tip) (m06)
- Central venous catheter (CVC) uses: chemotherapy and hyperosmolar drugs, central venous pressure, monitoring blood volume (m07)
- CVC appearance: small (3 mm), uniformly opaque, no marker stripe (m07)
- CVC position: path behind the medial clavicle then descends to the right of the spine; tip in the SVC at the junction with the right atrium (T5-T6, about 2 vertebral bodies below the carina); IJ and subclavian veins join to brachiocephalic veins then SVC (m07, e01)
- CVC malposition: tip in the right atrium (arrhythmias) or internal jugular vein; wrong central venous pressure readings (m08)
- CVC complications: pneumothorax in up to 5% (more by subclavian route); kinks suggest perforation; arterial placement (pulsatile blood, follows aortic arch, does not descend right of spine) (m09, m10)
- After a failed CVC attempt obtain a chest film before trying the other side (avoid bilateral pneumothoraces) (m10)
- PICC uses: long-term access (months), chemotherapy/antibiotics, frequent sampling; inserted via antecubital vein (m12)
- PICC position: tip in the SVC (may be axillary vein); small, hard to see; tips migrate over time; thrombosis from small lumen (m12)
- Pulmonary artery (Swan-Ganz) catheter uses: hemodynamics, cardiac versus noncardiac pulmonary edema; looks like a CVC but longer (m13)
- PA catheter position: tip in proximal right or left pulmonary artery within 2 cm of the hila; balloon inflated only for measurements; wedge pressure reflects left atrial pressure (m13)
- PA catheter complications: pulmonary infarction (most common significant), peripheral tip raises risk, pseudoaneurysm (new consolidation/mass at tip with hemoptysis) (m14)
- Hemodialysis (multiple lumen) catheter: large-bore, central stripe, at least two lumens, arterial (withdrawal) port proximal to venous port to limit recirculation; tip SVC and/or right atrium; right IJ route lowest clotting; infection and thrombosis late (m15)
- Chest tube: removes air or fluid; wide bore, marker stripe broken at side-hole; inserted at upper rib margin (nerve and vessels run under the rib); anterosuperior for pneumothorax, posteroinferior for effusion, usually works anywhere (m16, e02)
- Chest tube malposition: side-hole outside the chest wall causes air leak and subcutaneous emphysema; tube in a fissure drains poorly; kinks (m17, e02)
- Chest tube serious complications: intercostal artery bleeding, liver or spleen laceration, re-expansion pulmonary edema (m17, e02)
- Pacemaker: indications; generator subcutaneous in left anterior chest wall plus lead(s) via subclavian vein (m18, e03)
- Pacemaker leads: one tip at the right ventricular apex (left of spine on frontal, anterior on lateral); dual: right atrium and RV; triple adds coronary sinus; smooth curves, no kinks (m18, e03)
- Pacemaker/AICD complications: pneumothorax infrequent, lead fracture (generator, venous access site, tip), perforation with tamponade, Twiddler syndrome, ectopic lead (hepatic vein), migration (m19, e03)
- AICD: prevents sudden death (VF/VT); thicker, more opaque coil segment distinguishes it; one lead in SVC/brachiocephalic vein, another in RV apex; complications: dislodgement, fracture (m20, e03)
- Intra-aortic balloon pump: uses; balloon inflates in diastole, deflates in systole; tip marker in proximal descending aorta distal to left subclavian origin (about 2 cm below top of arch); too proximal: stroke; too distal: less effective; dissection (m21)
- Nasogastric tube: uses; wide (1 cm) with stripe broken at side-hole about 10 cm from tip; tip and side-holes about 10 cm into stomach past the EG junction; EG junction near left hemidiaphragm/spine junction (m22, e01)
- NGT malposition: most commonly malpositioned tube; coiling in esophagus most common; trachea/bronchus; rare perforation (cervical esophagus); reflux esophagitis/stricture; confirm by film before use (m23, e01)
- Feeding (Dobbhoff) tube: nutrition; ideal tip postpyloric (duodenum/jejunum) though stomach is common; weighted metallic tip; airway placement; guidewire not reinserted; confirm before feeding (m24)
- Case quiz: left subclavian CVC wandered into the left IJ; ETT tip less than 2 cm from carina, withdraw slightly (m04, m08)
- Online valve replacements: aortic valve above, mitral below the line from carina to anterior cardiophrenic sulcus; mitral left of spine/posterior; tricuspid vertical/anterior; pulmonic more superior (m25)
- Online devices: LVAD (continuous flow, no pulse; inflow in LV apex, outflow to ascending aorta) and Impella (femoral, across aortic valve into LV, temporary) (m26)
- Online devices: left atrial appendage clip (over 90% of AF thrombi), implantable loop recorder, coronary stent (MRI check), tissue expander, PEG tube (contrast in or outside lumen) (m27)

### Chapter 10: Recognizing Other Diseases of the Chest
26 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 43 checklist items

- Compartments on the lateral film: anterior (back of sternum to front of heart/great vessels), middle (heart, great vessel origins, trachea, main bronchi, nodes), posterior (back of heart to front of spine, plus paravertebral gutters); superior mediastinum above the aortic arch is merged with another (m01, e01)
- CT classification: prevascular, visceral, paravertebral; compartments have no true boundaries, so big masses are hard to assign (m01, e01)
- Mediastinal versus lung mass clues: surrounded by lung on both views means lung; mediastinal margins sharper; mediastinal masses displace/compress trachea or esophagus; CT is more accurate (m02)
- Anterior mediastinal masses, the "four T's": thyroid, terrible lymphoma, thymoma, teratoma (m03, e01)
- Substernal thyroid goiter: most common anterior mass in practice; multinodular; only one that routinely deviates the trachea (above the arch); classically not below the top of the arch; 3 of 4 anterior to trachea, 25% posterior (mostly right) (m03, e01)
- Goiter imaging: radioisotope scan is first choice; CT contiguous with thyroid, calcification, avid mottled contrast enhancement (m03)
- Lymphadenopathy: most common mediastinal mass overall (lymphoma, metastases, sarcoid, TB); anterior nodes most common in nodular sclerosing Hodgkin; lobulated/polycyclic border; node over 1 cm short axis; usually homogeneous, may be heterogeneous (necrosis, hemorrhage) (m04, e01)
- Hodgkin lymphoma: painless neck nodes; nodes above diaphragm; mediastinal adenopathy bilateral and asymmetric, with asymmetric hilar nodes; alcohol-induced node pain classical but uncommon (m04)
- Sarcoid versus lymphoma (Table 10.3): bilateral hilar plus right paratracheal nodes, peripheral bronchopulmonary nodes, effusion about 5%, anterior nodes uncommon versus mediastinal-dominant, asymmetric central hilar nodes, effusion about 30%, anterior nodes common (m05)
- Thymoma: epithelial/lymphocytic neoplasm, middle-aged adults (older than teratoma), mostly benign; myasthenia gravis in about 35%, thymoma in about 15% of myasthenia; thymectomy helps; smooth or lobulated mass at junction of heart and great vessels, may calcify; thymus involutes after age 20 (m06)
- Teratoma: germ-cell tumor with all three layers; mostly benign, younger than thymoma, often found by chance; about 30% malignant; cystic, well-marginated, near great vessel origins; fat, cartilage, bone on CT; surgical excision (m06)
- Middle mediastinum: heart, great vessel origins, trachea, main bronchi, nodes; lymphadenopathy is the most common mass (Hodgkin, small cell carcinoma, metastases such as breast, mononucleosis, TB usually unilateral, SLE) (m07, e01)
- Posterior mediastinum (paravertebral): descending aorta, esophagus, nodes, extramedullary hematopoiesis, and above all neurogenic tumors (m08, e01)
- Neurogenic tumors: nerve sheath tumors (schwannoma) most common, almost always benign, ages 20-50, slow growing; ganglioneuroma and neuroblastoma usually malignant; sharp paravertebral mass, rib erosion, dumbbell lesion through the foramen (m08, e01)
- Neurofibromatosis type 1: neurofibromas, rib notching, posterior vertebral scalloping (dural ectasia), absent sphenoid wings, pseudarthroses, sharp-angled kyphoscoliosis (m09)
- Nodule under 3 cm, mass over 3 cm; solitary pulmonary nodule (SPN): the key question is most likely benign versus malignant; prior imaging helps; many incidental nodules on CT (m10, e02)
- CT nodule types: solid versus subsolid (pure ground-glass, part-solid); ground-glass keeps vessel and bronchial markings; persistent subsolid nodules may be adenocarcinoma or precursors; part-solid has higher malignancy rate than pure ground-glass (m10)
- Fleischner 2017 (adults over 35, incidental noncalcified nodules): solid under 6 mm no routine follow-up (low risk) or optional CT at 12 months (high risk); 6-8 mm CT at 6-12 months then 18-24 months; over 8 mm consider CT at 3 months, PET/CT or tissue sampling; stable 2 years solid or 5 years subsolid is likely benign; low versus high risk factors (m11, e02)
- Benign versus malignant signs: size (under 6 mm rarely malignant; over 5 cm 95% malignant), calcification (central, laminar or diffuse is benign), margin (lobulated, spiculated, shaggy malignant), growth (weeks suggests inflammation, none over 2 years benign), cell-type growth rates (m12, e02)
- Clinical symptoms (hemoptysis, weight loss, hoarseness) raise malignancy risk; about 50% of resected nodules in men over 50 with suspicious findings are malignant; PET with FDG, nodule must be over 1 cm (e02)
- Benign causes: granulomas (TB, histoplasmosis; homogeneous, laminated or target calcification), hamartoma (fat and popcorn calcification), rheumatoid nodules, fungal infections, arteriovenous malformation, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (m13, e02)
- Bronchogenic carcinoma: most common fatal cancer in US men, second in women; primary cancer is usually a solitary nodule/mass, metastases multiple nodules (m14)
- Cell types (Table 10.5): squamous (central, segmental/lobar bronchi, obstruction, grows fairly fast), adenocarcinoma (peripheral, slowest), small cell (central, neurosecretory granules, paraneoplastic Cushing/SIADH, highly aggressive, mostly metastatic at diagnosis), large cell (diagnosis of exclusion, large peripheral, extremely rapid) (m14, e02)
- Three ways of recognising lung cancer (tumor itself, bronchial obstruction effects, extension or metastasis); nodule/mass usually adenocarcinoma with spiculated margin; cavitation more often squamous with thick wall and nodular inner margin (m15, e02)
- Bronchial obstruction: most often squamous cell carcinoma; obstructive pneumonitis (consolidation, often not infected) or atelectasis with shifts of fissures/mediastinum (m15)
- Direct extension and metastases: ipsilateral hilar nodes; contralateral mediastinal nodes worsen prognosis; mediastinal adenopathy may be the only sign of small cell carcinoma; multiple nodules in adenocarcinoma; effusion often with lymphangitic spread; bone metastases mixed lytic and sclerotic (m16)
- Pancoast tumor (Box 10.1 and Case Quiz): apical mass, adenocarcinoma or squamous, destroys first three ribs, brachial plexus invasion (shoulder pain), Horner syndrome (cervical sympathetic chain), SVC obstruction on the right; poor prognosis (m17)
- Small cell carcinoma take-home: highly aggressive, central, peribronchial; majority already metastatic at presentation; paraneoplastic syndromes (SIADH, Cushing) (m14)
- Hematogenous metastases: multiple nodules of slightly different sizes (embolized at different times), sharply marginated, micronodular to cannonball; primary cannot be identified from the appearance; biopsy gives the origin (m18)
- Table 10.6 primaries: males colorectal, renal, head and neck, testicular/bladder, melanoma, sarcoma; females breast, colorectal, renal, cervical/endometrial, melanoma, sarcoma (m18)
- Lymphangitic carcinomatosis: looks like interstitial edema (Kerley B lines, thick fissures, effusion); unilateral or single-lobe pattern points to it rather than heart failure; primaries breast, lung, pancreas; adenocarcinoma commonest (m19)
- Pulmonary embolism: over 90% from leg deep veins above the popliteal; surgery, bed rest, cancer; most do not infarct (dual circulation) (m20, e03)
- Chest radiograph in PE: often abnormal but nonspecific (subsegmental atelectasis, small effusion, raised hemidiaphragm), high false-negative rate; classic Hampton hump, Westermark sign, knuckle sign are infrequent (m20, e03)
- PE imaging path: normal radiograph then V/Q scan; abnormal radiograph then CT; CT-PA sensitivity over 90%, replaced V/Q in COPD or abnormal film (m21, e03)
- CT-PA findings and extras: filling defects in contrast-enhanced arteries; saddle embolus; delayed images of pelvic/leg veins; other diagnoses seen; triple rule-out (coronary disease, aortic dissection, PE) (m21, e03)
- COPD: airflow obstruction from chronic bronchitis (clinical, productive cough) or emphysema (pathological, permanent enlargement and destruction of airspaces beyond terminal bronchioles) (m22)
- Emphysema imaging: radiograph hyperinflation with flat diaphragm (especially lateral) is most reliable, increased retrosternal space, hyperlucency, pulmonary artery prominence; CT low-density areas without walls, extent and bullectomy planning (m22)
- Emphysema types: centriacinar (smoking, upper lobes, most common), panacinar (lower zones, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency), paraseptal (bullae, pneumothorax, no airflow obstruction) (m23)
- Air-containing lesions: bulla over 1 cm, wall under 1 mm, emphysema, apices, vanishing lung; bleb small, apical, pneumothorax; cyst wall under 3 mm (pneumatocele after Staphylococcus/Pneumocystis); cavity wall 3 mm to cm from necrosis (m24)
- Fluid in these lesions: from infection, hemorrhage, liquefaction; air-fluid level on horizontal beam; low CT number versus solid tumor; infected bulla patients less ill than lung abscess (m24)
- Cavity differential (Table 10.7): carcinoma thick wall with nodular inner margin; tuberculosis thin wall (upper lobe) with smooth inner margin; abscess thick wall with smooth inner margin and air-fluid level (m25)
- Bronchiectasis: localized irreversible dilatation; necrotizing infection (Staphylococcus, Klebsiella), lower lobes; cystic fibrosis, Kartagener syndrome, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, Swyer-James; productive cough with hemoptysis (m26)
- Bronchiectasis imaging: radiograph tram-tracks, cystic lesions up to 2 cm, tubular densities; CT is the study of choice: signet ring sign (bronchus larger than artery), failure to taper (most sensitive), cylindrical type least severe; cystic fibrosis upper lobes (m26)

### Chapter 11: Recognizing Adult Heart Disease

- Three reasons the heart shadow looks big: pericardial effusion, extracardiac factors, true cardiomegaly (e01)

- Plain films are poor for pericardial effusion (cannot tell fluid from heart); ultrasound is the first choice, CT shows small effusions; flask or water-bottle shape (m02, e01)
- Causes of pericardial effusion (Box 11.1): heart failure, infection, metastases, uremia, lupus, trauma, postpericardiotomy (m02)
- Extracardiac causes of apparent cardiomegaly (Table 11.1): portable supine AP film is the most common; poor inspiration (8-9 posterior ribs = adequate); obesity, pregnancy, ascites; pectus excavatum; rotation (m03, e01)
- Cardiothoracic ratio: normal under 50%; the heart looks slightly larger on AP than on PA (m04, e01)
- Portable AP films: borderline = normal, significantly enlarged = enlarged, touching the left lateral chest wall = definitely enlarged (m04, e01)
- Lateral film: posterior heart border overlapping the thoracic spine is a confirmatory sign of an enlarged silhouette (m05, e01)
- Congestive heart failure: common; most frequent causes are coronary artery disease and hypertension; other causes (m06)
- Two radiographic patterns of heart failure: interstitial edema and alveolar edema (they overlap) (m06, m07, e02)
- Four key signs of interstitial edema (Box 11.2): septal thickening (Kerley lines), peribronchial cuffing, fluid in the fissures, pleural effusions (m07, e02)
- Kerley B lines: short, thin, horizontal lines at the bases abutting the pleura; thickened interlobular septa at wedge pressure about 15 mm Hg; chronic B lines after fibrosis (m08, e02)
- Kerley A lines run out from the hila and do not reach the periphery; C lines doubtful (m08)
- Peribronchial cuffing: thickened bronchial walls seen on-end as small doughnut rings (m09)
- Fluid in the fissures: normally pencil-thin or invisible; thick and irregular in edema; may stay thick after repeated failure (m09)
- Pleural effusion in heart failure (wedge pressure about 20 mm Hg): usually bilateral; if unilateral usually right; isolated left effusion suggests other causes; laminar effusion (m10, e02)
- Alveolar edema (wedge pressure about 25 mm Hg): fluffy patchy airspace shadows, bat-wing/butterfly pattern sparing the outer third, effusions; rapid failure such as myocardial infarction (m11, e02)
- Cardiomegaly is not a sensitive sign of failure; cephalization is meaningful only on an upright film; edema clears in hours to days (periphery to centre) and the film lags behind the patient (m12)
- Noncardiogenic edema causes: increased capillary permeability (ARDS: sepsis, uremia, DIC, smoke, near-drowning), volume overload, lymphangitic carcinomatosis, high altitude, neurogenic, reexpansion, drug overdose (m14)
- ARDS course: films normal for 24-36 hours, then edema; stable at 5-7 days, improving at about 2 weeks, clearing may take months; severe hypoxia (m13)
- Cardiogenic vs noncardiogenic edema (Table 11.3): effusions, Kerley B lines and enlarged heart are common in cardiogenic; normal heart and normal wedge pressure (under 12) favour noncardiogenic (m14, e02)
- Reexpansion pulmonary edema: unilateral, after overly rapid expansion of a chronically collapsed lung; other causes of unilateral edema (m15)
- Hypertensive heart disease: 90% essential; left ventricular hypertrophy at the expense of the lumen, heart normal or slightly large early; aorta uncoils; complications (m16)
- Mitral stenosis: rheumatic, symptoms of left failure, valve area under one third of normal, left atrial enlargement, cephalization, pulmonary arterial hypertension and right failure (m17)
- Pulmonary arterial hypertension: normal mean pressure about 15 mm Hg; idiopathic or secondary (emphysema, mitral stenosis, emboli); pruning; main pulmonary artery over 3 cm and larger than the aorta (m18)
- Aortic stenosis: chest pain, heart failure, syncope; bicuspid, degenerative or rheumatic; heart normal early (hypertrophy); poststenotic dilatation of the ascending aorta; valve calcification on CT (m19)
- Cardiomyopathy definition (excludes ischemic and valvular causes); dilated type: ejection fraction under 40%, 90% of cases, large heart, echo, MRI most accurate (m20)
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: familial 50%, leading cause of sudden death in the young and athletes, asymmetric or concentric LV thickening, obstruction with systolic anterior motion, echo or gated MRI (m21)
- Restrictive cardiomyopathy: least common, stiff myocardium, infiltrative causes (amyloid, sarcoid, radiation), heart not enlarged, signs of congestive failure (m22)
- Restrictive cardiomyopathy vs constrictive pericarditis: pericardium normal (under 4 mm) vs thickened or calcified; constrictive pericarditis is surgically curable (m22)
- Aortic aneurysm: over 50% enlargement; thoracic aorta over 4 cm; normal ascending under 3.5 cm and descending under 3 cm; atherosclerosis most common in descending; often asymptomatic (m23, e03)
- Aneurysm management and imaging: 5-6 cm risks rupture, growth over 1 cm per year; appearance by site; fusiform or saccular; wall calcification and clot; contrast CT (m23, e03)
- Aortic dissection: Stanford type A (ascending) and B (descending); tear and dissection through the media; predisposing conditions; abrupt ripping pain (m24, e03)
- Radiographic signs of dissection (unreliable alone): widened mediastinum (overread on supine films, present in only about 1 in 4), left apical cap, loss of aortic knob, trachea/esophagus pushed right, left pleural effusion (m24)
- Dissection on CT/MRI: intimal flap separates true (smaller) and false lumen; type A surgical, type B medical; transesophageal ultrasound also used (m25, e03)
- Coronary artery disease: leading cause of death worldwide; plaque, calcium, rupture, spasm, emboli; coronary angiography is the gold standard (m26)
- Imaging coronary disease: cardiac CT (high negative predictive value, calcium score without contrast), MRI (scar, contractility), SPECT (stress/rest ischemia), CT complications of infarction (m26)

### Chapter 12: Recognizing the Normal Abdomen and Pelvis: Conventional Radiographs
21 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 24 checklist items

- Four-step approach to the abdominal film: overall gas pattern, extraluminal air, abnormal calcifications, soft-tissue masses (m01)
- Bowel gas is swallowed air (little from fermentation); distended loops (air fills the lumen) are normal, dilated loops (beyond normal size) are abnormal (m02, m03)
- Stomach almost always contains air, unless recent vomiting or a nasogastric tube on suction (m02, e01)
- Small bowel: air in two or three nondilated loops; normal under 2.5 cm; maximal dilatation about 5 cm; large bowel can dilate to several times that (m04)
- Large bowel: air in the rectum or sigmoid almost always; colon distends to about barium-enema size; stool recognised as small gas bubbles in soft-tissue density (m05, e02)
- Aerophagia: swallowed air, belching and bloating; many polygonal air-filled loops, none dilated (m03)
- Normal air-fluid levels (Table 12.1): stomach yes; small bowel two or three; colon none or very few; many colonic levels after an enema or with anticholinergic drugs (m06, m07, e01)
- Large vs small bowel: large bowel peripheral with haustra that do not cross the lumen and are widely spaced; small bowel central with valvulae that cross the lumen and are close together (m09)
- Acute abdominal series: supine abdomen, prone abdomen (or lateral rectum), upright abdomen (or left lateral decubitus), upright chest (or supine chest); what each looks for (Table 12.2) (m10, e01)
- Supine view (scout): gas pattern, calcifications and masses; vertical beam; no view substitutes for it (m10)
- Prone or lateral rectum view: gas in rectosigmoid and the posterior ascending and descending colon; no rectal gas can be a clue to mechanical obstruction (m12)
- Upright abdomen: free air and air-fluid levels; horizontal beam; left lateral decubitus substitute (left side down, free air over the liver on the right); right decubitus pitfall (m11, e01)
- Upright or supine chest in the series: free air under the diaphragm, basal pneumonia that mimics an acute abdomen, pleural effusions (pancreatitis left, subphrenic abscess right, ovarian tumours); supine chest may miss small free air (m13, e01)
- Air-fluid level needs air, fluid and a horizontal beam; not seen on vertical-beam (supine) films (m08, e01)
- Phleboliths: calcified venous thrombi in the pelvis, older women, lucent centre, mimic ureteral calculi (m14, e02)
- Calcified rib cartilage: amorphous speckled arc, more common in women, over 35, can mimic renal or biliary stones (m15)
- Soft-tissue organs and masses: recognised directly (borders outlined by fat or air) or indirectly (displacement of air-filled bowel) (m16, e03)
- Liver: displaces gas from the right upper quadrant; Riedel lobe is a normal variant; hepatomegaly shown by bowel displaced to the iliac crest and across the midline (m17, e03)
- Spleen: about 12 cm, not below the 12th rib, about the size of the left kidney; enlarged if well below the 12th rib or if it pushes the stomach bubble toward or across the midline (m18, e03)
- Kidneys: outlined by perirenal fat; about four lumbar vertebral bodies (10-14 cm); the right is lower because of the liver; only very large kidneys or masses show (m19, e03)
- Urinary bladder: dome outlined by fat; oval, base above the symphysis; enlargement displaces bowel upward; bladder outlet obstruction (prostate) makes a pelvic mass more likely a bladder in men (m20, e02)
- Uterus: atop the bladder dome with a fat lucency between; about 8 x 4 x 6 cm; ultrasound is best; psoas muscles may be seen and their absence is not a reliable sign (m21, e02)
- Case quiz: leukemia with hepatosplenomegaly shown by displaced stomach bubble, depressed splenic and hepatic flexures; bowel displacement suggests organomegaly (m18)
- CT, ultrasound and MRI have essentially replaced plain films for organomegaly and masses; plain films remain the easy first study (m16, e03)

### Chapter 13: Recognizing the Normal Abdomen and Pelvis: Computed Tomography
25 multiple-choice, 3 essays, 25 checklist items

- CT in abdominal pain (about 10% of emergency visits); exploratory surgery now rare; guiding principle: maximize density differences with intravenous and oral contrast (m01)
- Intravenous contrast: enhanced scans give more information; parameters tailored (e.g. vessels vs parenchyma); nonionic low-osmolar iodine, opacifies high-flow tissue, looks white, excreted by the kidneys (m02, e01)
- Contrast reactions: mild (warmth, nausea, local itching or hives) need no treatment; idiosyncratic allergic-like reactions; severe in about 0.01-0.04% (death 1 in 200,000-300,000) (m03)
- Higher risk (asthma, severe allergies, prior reaction): steroids, diphenhydramine, cimetidine; shellfish allergy is unrelated; oral contrast safe after a prior reaction (m04)
- Renal function: eGFR under 30 or creatinine 1.5 or more, not on dialysis: intravenous saline may reduce contrast-associated acute kidney injury (m05)
- Oral contrast: defines the bowel; use has diminished; not routine in chest CT; divided doses; not used for trauma, the stone search or vascular studies (m06, e02)
- Oral contrast agents: dilute barium sulfate (most used); water-soluble iodine-based (Gastrografin) if perforation is a concern; rectal contrast; Foley catheter for the bladder (m07, e02)
- Table 13.1: IV contrast for CT-PA, mass and staging, trauma, abdominal pain, aneurysm or dissection; no IV contrast for calcium scoring, stone search; oral contrast for most nontraumatic pain, IBD, abscess, perforation or fistula; radiologist tailors the study (m06, m08, e02)
- Patient preparation (Table 13.2): renal function check before contrast; fasting for upper abdominal US; full bladder for pelvic US; MRI safety history (pacemaker, clips, implants, claustrophobia); barium bowel preparation; sip of water for oral medications (m09)
- Modalities compared (Table 13.3): radiography, ultrasound (gallbladder, aortic aneurysm screening, female pelvis, ascites; operator dependent), CT (modality of choice, radiation, contrast issues), MRI (soft-tissue contrast, cost, availability, devices) (m10)
- Radiation dose of CT depends on equipment, x-ray energy and patient size; dose-reduction measures; benefit must outweigh risk; history and examination guide the choice of study (m11)
- Viewing convention: patient's right on your left; supine patient with anterior at the top of the image (m12, e03)
- Liver blood supply: 80% portal vein, 20% hepatic artery; enhances mostly in the portal venous phase; hepatic veins drain to the IVC (m13, e01)
- Liver lobes by external appearance: right (anterior, posterior), left (medial, lateral), caudate; fissure with falciform ligament and ligamentum teres separates medial and lateral left segments (m14)
- Couinaud: eight functional segments, each with its own portal branch, artery, bile duct and hepatic vein drainage; basis for segmental resection; not the same as the lobes (m15)
- Normal liver on CT: smooth and homogeneous, at least as dense as the spleen without contrast; 15 cm or less in height (not through a Riedel lobe); transverse 20-26 cm; volume about 1500 cm3 (m16, e03)
- Bare area of the liver: no peritoneal covering, attached to the diaphragm; ascites cannot pass between liver and lung (separates ascites from pleural effusion) (m17)
- Spleen: about 12 cm, not below the 12th rib, size of the left kidney; patchy early enhancement is normal; lobulations are not lacerations (m18, e03)
- Pancreas: retroperitoneal and oblique; head 3 cm, body 2.5, tail 2; length 12-15 cm; duct 3-4 mm; fatty infiltration with age; relations to splenic vessels and SMV (m19, e03)
- Kidneys: retroperitoneal, perirenal space and fasciae; about 11 cm, left minimally larger; right renal artery behind the IVC; left renal vein in front of the aorta; veins anterior to arteries (m20, e03)
- Renal phases: corticomedullary 30-70 s, nephrographic 80-100 s, excretory 5-10 min; what each shows (m21, e01)
- Kidneys excrete contrast; urine becomes opacified (delayed imaging); vicarious excretion if the kidneys fail (m22, e01)
- Bowel on CT: distension and opacification needed; small bowel 2.5 cm or less, wall under 3 mm; colon wall under 3 mm distended and under 5 mm collapsed; cecum identified by terminal ileum and ileocecal valve (m23, e02)
- Urinary bladder: extraperitoneal; relations to prostate, rectum, vagina and uterus; ureters enter at the trigone; wall 5 mm or less when distended (m24)
- Case quiz: pulsatile abdominal mass: ultrasound first; noncontrast CT if unsuitable; CT or MR angiography for definitive diagnosis (m25)
